How text-to-sound encoding works

Compose

Write a text payload or choose the prayer label. Messages are limited to 512 UTF-8 bytes.

Encode

ZionCore converts the payload into a versioned binary frame with a checksum and binary frequency-shift keying (BFSK) tones.

Transfer nearby

Play the audio through a speaker or move the generated WAV file to another device with a compatible microphone.

Verify and recover

The receiver reports text only after the packet structure and checksum pass validation.

The current protocol uses 1,800 Hz and 2,400 Hz tones at 50 bits per second in a 48 kHz WAV. The CRC checksum can detect accidental changes; it does not authenticate the sender or encrypt the message.

A practical sound communication experiment

Acoustic messaging makes a small digital packet audible. It is useful for exploring how information can be represented in sound, learning about modulation, and trying a simple device-to-device transfer in the same room.

Speaker and microphone frequency response, room noise, distance, and playback volume can affect recovery. Keep devices nearby and use a comfortable volume. The protocol is intended for a compatible local receiver, not long-range transmission or private communication.

Messages are encoded and decoded in the browser. ZionCore does not send the packet to its interpretation service, store it in cloud history, or claim that a checksum proves who transmitted it.

Acoustic message questions

Can I encode a prayer into sound?

Yes. Choose the prayer payload label and enter a short prayer. It is converted into the same acoustic packet format as text.

Is the acoustic message encrypted?

No. The current packet includes a checksum for accidental corruption detection, but does not encrypt or authenticate the sender. Do not use it for confidential information.

Can any speaker or microphone decode it?

Many devices may work at close range, but hardware and room conditions vary. A matching ZionCore receiver must capture enough of the packet for the checksum to pass.
